HEXAGRAM LABEL: A
PRIMARY: 48 水風井 -> CHANGED: 28 澤風大過 (Line 4)
MUTUAL: 火澤睽

1. SITUATION DESCRIPTION
Water above wind: Jing, the Well. The one hexagram named for a piece of civic infrastructure. Its judgment is remarkable: "改邑不改井" — the town may be moved but the well is not; it neither decreases nor increases. The use trigram feeds the body trigram: the situation nourishes the subject.

2. KEY ACTORS / FORCES
- Kan as body: water, the thing drawn up, the depth.
- Xun as use: wood, the bucket and rope, the mechanism of drawing.
- The fourth line is correct in place but unanswered, and rests on a firm line — soft over hard, the unstable arrangement. Its text: "井甃，无咎" — the well is lined with brick; no blame. 甃 is the masonry lining of the shaft. Maintenance work: nobody drinks during it, and nothing is produced.

3. PROCESS NARRATIVE
The Jing sequence is the cleanest ascending arc in the book — mud, leaking jar, cleaned but undrunk, lined, cold and clear, drawn without cover. The fourth line is the repair rung. The well is out of service while the shaft is bricked; the verdict is 无咎, no blame, which is the book's phrase for work that is correct without being rewarding. The mutual hexagram Kui (睽, Opposition) marks divergence at the centre. The transformation into Da Guo (大過, Great Exceeding) — the ridgepole bending under load — introduces structural strain: a beam weak at both ends carrying too much in the middle.

4. QUALITATIVE OUTCOME CHARACTER
Necessary maintenance performed correctly and without visible payoff, resolving into a structure under excessive load. The flavour is of doing the unglamorous repair that has to be done, and of the strain afterwards being greater than the repair anticipated.

5. SPECIFIC IMAGERY / METAPHORS
- A well being relined with brick: the shaft sound again, no water drawn meanwhile.
- The town that moves while the well stays: infrastructure outlasting the population it serves.
- No blame: correctness without reward, the most neutral verdict in the vocabulary.
- The ridgepole sagging: a load-bearing member past its comfortable limit.

6. WARNINGS / SURPRISES
Jing is the hexagram of what does not change, and the fourth line is where the unchanging thing requires work to stay unchanging. The position's instability suggests the repair is happening under pressure rather than at leisure. The surprise is Da Guo: maintenance completed and the structure immediately overloaded — the fix does not buy the slack it was supposed to.
